A competition rocket is two systems sharing one tube. The **outer** parts give it shape and keep it flying straight. The **inner** parts make it climb, measure the flight, come down safely, and protect the egg. Learn them separately so you can talk about a design without mixing up a fin problem with a motor problem.

## Inner components

These ride inside the airframe. You may not see them from ten feet away, but they decide altitude, flight time, and whether the egg survives.

### Motors

A model rocket <GlossaryTerm id="motor">motor</GlossaryTerm> is a solid-propellant charge in a cardboard-and-clay casing. It burns in three phases:

1. **Thrust** — Propellant ignites and burns from the core outward. Hot gas exits the nozzle and pushes the rocket up. In a code like **F42** or **C6**, the letter is the total-impulse class (how much energy the motor delivers). The number after it is average thrust in Newtons.
2. **Delay** — After the propellant is spent, a slow-burning delay charge takes over. It makes smoke, not thrust, and gives the rocket time to coast toward <GlossaryTerm id="apogee">apogee</GlossaryTerm>. In **C6-6**, that final **6** is seconds of delay. A delay drill can lengthen the wait before ejection; longer delay can mean a higher coast before the parachute pops — use it carefully.
3. **Ejection** — At the end of the delay, an ejection charge sends a pulse of hot gas forward into the body tube. That pressure pops the recovery bay open so the parachute can deploy.

<MotorPhases className="w-full my-6" />

For ARC, teams may only fly **class F** motors. Each motor on the approved list has its own thrust curve — check the seller and the approved-motors list before you commit a design to a motor code.

### Altimeter

The <GlossaryTerm id="altimeter">altimeter</GlossaryTerm> measures altitude from changing air pressure. Many units also log useful extras such as apogee height, speed, and related flight data. For ARC, only four altimeters are permitted:

- PerfectFlite Pnut
- PerfectFlite FireFly
- Jolly Logic Altimeter One
- Jolly Logic Altimeter Two

Build or buy a hard case so the unit stays secure. Mount it where **vent holes** let outside air reach the sensor — without airflow, pressure readings (and your score) go wrong.

<AltimeterBay className="w-full my-6" />

<Callout tone="go" title="Vent tip">
  Small, clean vent holes beat a sealed bay every time. The altimeter needs the
  same air the rocket is flying through.
</Callout>

### Parachutes

After ejection, the <GlossaryTerm id="parachute">parachute</GlossaryTerm> slows descent. For ARC, parachutes must be **cotton**. Larger diameters catch more air and stretch flight time; if you are overshooting the duration window, some teams carefully cut a spill hole in the canopy to come down faster — that is a risk trade, not a free fix.

### Nomex (fire blanket)

<GlossaryTerm id="nomex">Nomex</GlossaryTerm> is fire-resistant fabric that shields the parachute, shock cord, and nearby wiring from ejection heat and embers. ARC does not mandate one brand or size — it does require genuine fire-resistant fabric, not tissue paper or ordinary cloth.

Place Nomex **between** the ejection charge and the recovery gear. It blocks contact with hot gas; it does not have to form an airtight seal. Pack it loosely enough that the chute can still unfurl the instant the bay opens.

<NomexShield className="w-full my-6" />

### Egg protection case

The egg case cradles the raw egg payload through launch loads, deployment shock, and landing. Soft cushioning must surround the egg on **all sides**, not just hold it in a hard cup. Officials inspect the egg before and after qualifying flights, so the case must open for insertion and removal. Keeping the egg mass in a fixed place also keeps your <GlossaryTerm id="center-of-gravity">center of gravity</GlossaryTerm> predictable.

## Outer components

These are the parts you see on the pad: tube, nose, fins. They set drag, strength, and stability.

### Body tube

The <GlossaryTerm id="body-tube">body tube</GlossaryTerm> is the main structural cylinder. It houses the motor mount, recovery system, altimeter, and payload bay, and it is the mounting surface for fins and rail buttons.

For ARC:

- Diameter must be the **same from nose to tail**
- That diameter must be at least **47 mm (1.85 in)**
- Overall rocket length must be at least **650 mm**

Use lightweight, **non-metal** materials (cardboard, phenolic, fiberglass, and similar) per the NAR Safety Code. Reinforce motor-mount and coupler areas — those spots take the hardest ejection and landing loads.

### Nose cone

The <GlossaryTerm id="nose-cone">nose cone</GlossaryTerm> cuts drag and often anchors the shock cord. It can also carry nose weight for stability or a tracker. Shape and material are flexible under ARC rules, but it must be lightweight and non-metal. It needs to separate cleanly at ejection (unless your design intentionally keeps it fixed).

Do **pull tests** on the bench: attach the shock cord and yank the nose off at the angle and force ejection would apply. Repeat. A sticky shoulder on the bench becomes a failed deployment on the pad.

### Fins

<GlossaryTerm id="fins">Fins</GlossaryTerm> keep the <GlossaryTerm id="center-of-pressure">center of pressure</GlossaryTerm> behind the center of gravity so the rocket flies straight. Count and planform are team choices; material must stay lightweight and non-metal. Aim for a solid stability margin (teams often target roughly **1.5–2 calibers**). Mount fins straight and strong — a cocked or loose fin can end the flight.

You can **3D print** fins: model them in Fusion 360, Tinkercad, or export from OpenRocket for your exact tube diameter, then print in something like PLA at low infill (often 15–20%) to save mass. Print a test fin first and flex it. Orient layers along the fin length, not across the root, so landing shock is less likely to snap it.

## OpenRocket — design before you cut

<GlossaryTerm id="openrocket">OpenRocket</GlossaryTerm> is free software for building a full virtual rocket — diameters, lengths, nose shape, fins, motor mount, recovery, and payload mass — then simulating the flight before you glue anything.

What it gives an ARC team:

- **Altitude and time predictions** before you burn motors — try motors, weights, and drag changes in an afternoon instead of a weekend of rebuilds
- **Stability checks** — it places CG and CP and reports margin in calibers so unstable designs fail on screen, not on the pad
- **A real motor database** — thrust curves for commercial motors, including ARC-approved options
- **Descent tuning** — match parachute size to landing weight so you protect the egg and stay inside the duration window
- **Cheap iteration** — change nose weight, fin span, or delay in a text field instead of a full rebuild

One hard limit: it is a **simulation**. Real drag, wind, glue joints, finish, and exact mass will move your flight away from the plot. Smart teams fly, compare the altimeter to OpenRocket, then adjust the model’s drag coefficient so later predictions match reality.

## Put it together

When something goes wrong on a flight, name the system first:

- **Motor / delay** problems show up as wrong apogee timing or early/late deployment
- **Altimeter / vents** problems show up as nonsense altitude numbers
- **Recovery / Nomex** problems show up as burned chutes, failed deployment, or long/short duration
- **Egg case** problems show up as cracked shells despite a soft-looking landing
- **Airframe / fins / nose** problems show up as crooked flight, broken tubes, or stuck separation
- **OpenRocket mismatch** means your model’s drag or mass is lying — calibrate it from data

You do not need to memorize every brand name on day one. You do need the map: outer shape for flight path, inner systems for climb, measure, recover, and protect.
